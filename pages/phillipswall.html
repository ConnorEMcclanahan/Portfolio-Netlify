Understanding the Visitor Feedback Problem

Phillips Museum visitors were already leaving opinions on physical sticky notes, but the feedback was difficult to organize, compare, or revisit over time. The project asked how those scattered responses could become an interactive record of changing public perspectives.

The concept focused on turning handwritten notes into structured, searchable insight without removing the human character of the original feedback. The system needed to help visitors see how their thoughts compared with others while giving curators a clearer view of recurring themes and shifts in sentiment.

Data Preparation and AI Pipeline

The pipeline digitizes sticky-note responses, transcribes handwritten text, translates it when needed, and prepares each response for categorization and visualization. During development, the team improved the earlier OCR approach by moving to OpenAI-based text recognition for more consistent results.

This stage made the difference between a static collection of notes and a usable data source. Better transcription meant more reliable sentiment analysis, clearer categories, and fewer misleading patterns in the final timeline.

Requirements and Responsible AI

The timeline needed real-time updates, AI-powered categorization, sentiment and emotion analysis, and predictive insights based on feedback over time. It also needed to remain understandable for visitors and useful for researchers and educators who would interpret the data.

Responsible use was part of the product definition. Visitor notes may contain private or sensitive information, handwriting can be misread by AI, and automated classifications should support human interpretation rather than replace it. Clear terms, content moderation, and transparency about how notes are processed are essential next steps.

Exploring the Interactive Timeline

Early concepts explored how visitor opinions could be placed on a timeline and grouped into themes. The interface needed to make change visible without turning the exhibit into another dense dashboard. The most important design question was how to let people move between individual responses, recurring topics, and broader emotional patterns.

Feedback from the museum and project reviews pushed the design toward clearer visual hierarchy, more accessible language, and stronger emphasis on where a visitor's input appears in the larger conversation.

Prototype and Museum Testing

The prototype connected the visitor input flow to a visual representation of the collected responses. The group tested the concept at the Phillips Museum and used visitor reactions to improve the interface for people with different levels of technical confidence.

Impact and Next Steps

The project offers visitors a more active way to engage with an exhibit and gives curators evidence they can use for longer-term planning. Instead of treating feedback as a one-time archive, the timeline makes public perception visible as something that evolves.

The main risks are privacy, inaccurate interpretation of handwriting or emotion, and over-reliance on automated conclusions. Future work should strengthen consent and data-retention language, improve human review tools, and test whether the visualizations help visitors understand the data without oversimplifying it.
