Research Approach

To better understand the pain points and unmet needs of elderly users and the clinical requirements behind the product, I conducted foundational research using a mixed-methods approach. The goals for my research process were to explore patterns in how people use health monitoring apps, identify the barriers they face, and understand what would help them feel confident using an ECG sensor at home.

The research revealed several important patterns. Users found many health apps too complicated, wanted clear confirmation that their device was working, and needed larger text and simple visual guidance. These findings directly informed the accessibility standards, status feedback, onboarding flow, and feature priorities used throughout the project.

Literature Review

I reviewed WCAG 2.1 AA standards, research into older adult interfaces, and medical device requirements before making design decisions. I focused on presbyopia, reduced contrast sensitivity, slower touch interactions, and different levels of digital confidence. This gave me a foundation for larger text, strong contrast, simple language, and touch targets of at least 44 by 44 pixels. It also clarified that the application should communicate device status, battery, and connection without interpreting ECG data or suggesting a diagnosis.

Stakeholder Interviews

I spoke with cardiologists, GPs, and company stakeholders about the clinical and technical requirements behind the interface. We discussed the value of longer monitoring periods, problems caused by leads detaching, movement affecting signal quality, and the importance of accurate sensor placement. These conversations clarified that the app could show whether the device was connected, recording, and working, but diagnostic interpretation had to remain with healthcare professionals. This shaped the status bar, onboarding language, placement guidance, and alerts.

Patient Interviews

I conducted in-depth interviews with elderly Holter monitor users aged 70 and above to understand what using a monitoring device felt like at home. Participants described physical discomfort, anxiety about whether the device had stopped working, and confusion caused by technical information. They were less interested in graphs than in simple answers about whether the device was working, whether readings were being recorded, and what to do if something went wrong. These insights informed the focus on reassurance, large text, simple navigation, and visual guidance.

Survey Analysis

I ran a quantitative survey with 50 respondents aged 18 to 75 and above to identify common problems with health monitoring apps and compare those results with the interviews. The strongest findings were that 54% found health apps too complicated, 14% of respondents aged 46 and above struggled with small text, and 70% wanted clear confirmation that their device was working. Respondents also preferred video demonstrations and step-by-step visual guidance for device placement. These results became direct design priorities rather than assumptions.

Internal Feedback

I reviewed the existing Figma designs with Diplora stakeholders before moving into prototyping. This helped me understand the intended product direction, identify where the interface did not reflect the accessibility and medical requirements, and connect the research to the practical constraints of the project. I used the feedback to refine the visual direction, simplify the most important flows, and decide which features belonged in the MVP, including the persistent status bar, accessible onboarding, and focused symptom logging flow.

Brand Identity

I helped shape Diplora's visual identity around medical credibility, reassurance, and ease of use. The design needed to feel trustworthy and calm without making the interface feel clinical or intimidating, especially for older adults using the app at home.

Accessible Design Standards

I translated the research into practical standards: minimum 18pt body text, at least 4.5:1 contrast, 44x44px touch targets, clear hierarchy, readable language, and icons supported by text labels.

Scalable Components

I established reusable patterns for buttons, cards, navigation, status feedback, spacing, and form controls. These components created a consistent foundation for the Flutter implementation and future development.

Using the MOSCOW method, I prioritized features based on accessibility and clinical validation requirements. Must-have features address the core needs identified in research.

Research Methods & Deliverables

These insights informed the focus on reassurance, large text, simple navigation, and visual guidance.

Survey Analysis: I ran a quantitative survey with 50 respondents aged 18 to 75 and above to identify common problems with health monitoring apps and compare those results with the interview findings. User Personas

Two primary user personas informed the design, representing the distinct needs of elderly patients and their healthcare providers.

Persona 1: Primary User

Represents elderly patients aged 65+ who prioritize device reassurance and simple operation over comprehensive medical data.

Persona 2: Secondary User

Healthcare professionals and caregivers who need diagnostic data access while ensuring elderly users have simplified, reassuring experiences.

Prototyping & User Testing

Conducted iterative user testing with elderly users (ages 56-70+) to validate accessibility-first design patterns. Six major design iterations addressed user feedback and expert recommendations.

What Worked Well

  • Large touch targets (44x44px) were easy to hit and navigate
  • "Device Working" confirmation provided essential reassurance
  • Simplified dashboard layout with only essential information
  • Text labels with icons improved clarity significantly
  • Simple three-block layout felt intuitive to elderly users

Key Iterations

  • Button label clarity: "Add Complaint" → "Log Symptom"
  • Icon addition: Added text labels to all navigation elements
  • Help button: Made visible on home screen for easy access
  • Battery display: De-emphasized to reduce visual clutter
  • Logbook: Simplified from tabs to unified list view
  • Device connection: Auto-start search matching familiar phone Bluetooth behavior

Design Iterations Based on Feedback

The design underwent six major iterations based on combined feedback from user testing, expert review, and internal stakeholder input. Each iteration was informed by real user behavior and expert guidance.

Iteration 1: Button Label Clarity

Issue: Test users confused by "Add Complaint"—Faye thought it meant complaining about the sensor itself

Change: Renamed to "Log Symptom" throughout the interface; "Detected Activity" → "Activity History"

Impact: Users immediately understood each button's purpose

Iteration 2: Icon Replacement

Issue: Placeholder icons didn't clearly indicate function; confusing for elderly users

Change: Replaced unclear icons with standard health app iconography; added text labels to all navigation items

Impact: Functions became immediately recognizable

Iteration 3: Help Button Addition

Issue: Mildred couldn't find instructions when needed; Ben requested "easy Help button"

Change: Added visible "Help" button to main navigation, always accessible from home screen

Impact: Users can access instructions at any point without navigating through settings

Iteration 4: Battery Display De-emphasis

Issue: Large battery indicator took significant space; Mildred said "It's too big"

Change: Reduced battery to small status indicator in persistent status bar; only draws attention when charging threshold reached

Impact: Refocused home screen hierarchy on actionable items, reduced visual clutter

Iteration 5: Logbook Simplification

Issue: Separate tabs for "Review Alerts" and "History" with filtering options was overcomplicated

Change: Combined into single unified list; actionable items emphasized, removed unnecessary filtering

Impact: Cleaner, less technical interface that's easier to navigate

High-Fidelity Prototypes

Designed high-fidelity prototypes based on feedback from low-fidelity testing. These detailed, interactive prototypes incorporated refined design elements and enhanced functionality, providing an accurate representation of the final product ready for user validation.
